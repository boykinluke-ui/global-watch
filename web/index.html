God reconciles sinners to himself through the life, death and resurrection of Jesus Christ.

The Bible’s mission does not begin with our plans. It begins with God’s grace: Christ saves, forms a people, and sends his followers to make disciples among all nations.

“But God demonstrates his own love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us.”

FOR THE CURIOUS, SKEPTICAL OR WOUNDED

Explore honestly

The Christian message makes real claims about Jesus. You can examine them without pretending to believe; take hard questions and experiences of harm seriously.

Who is Jesus?

The New Testament presents Jesus as Israel’s Messiah and the Son of God, who proclaimed God’s kingdom, taught and healed, was crucified, and was raised. Christians confess that Jesus is Lord; readers can examine the earliest Christian sources and ask what the claims mean.

Read in context: Mark 1–2 · John 1:1–18 · 1 Corinthians 15:1–8.

What is the Bible?

The Bible is a collection of writings across many centuries, genres and historical settings. Christians receive it as Scripture and read its diverse books in relation to God’s work, centered for Christians on Jesus. Individual passages should be read in their literary and historical context.

BibleProject · How to Read the Bible ↗

Why do Christians believe Jesus saves?

Romans 3:21–26 presents righteousness as God’s gift through Jesus; Ephesians 2:1–10 describes salvation by grace through faith and a life of good works prepared by God. Christians differ on some explanations of atonement and salvation, while affirming that salvation is God’s work in Christ.

What about suffering and evil?

The Bible includes lament, protest and unanswered questions; it does not ask people to call suffering good. Christians differ in how they explain evil, but point to Jesus’ suffering and resurrection and hope for restoration. Listen carefully to anyone who has been harmed.

Read: Job 1–3 · Psalm 13 · John 11 · Romans 8:18–39.

Can the historical claims be examined?

Historical study can examine early Christian texts, their context and transmission, and the origins of resurrection belief. Historical methods can assess sources and explain arguments; they do not by themselves compel a theological conclusion. Christians and skeptics debate how to interpret the evidence.

Read: Luke 1:1–4 · 1 Corinthians 15:1–8 · Acts 17:11.

Why has the Church caused harm?

Abuse, coercion, hypocrisy and institutional failures should be named and addressed; they must not be excused by religious language. Distinguish Jesus’ teaching from people’s actions without using that distinction to evade responsibility. Hear affected people, protect the vulnerable and pursue accountability.

Read: Matthew 23 · Luke 10:25–37 · James 1:19–27.

How do Christians view other beliefs?

Christianity makes distinctive truth claims about Jesus and salvation; it does not teach that all religions are the same. Learn other traditions from their own teachers, avoid caricature, and treat people of every religion and none with dignity. Christians differ on how to describe religious freedom and dialogue.

Read: Acts 17:16–34 · 1 Peter 3:15–16.

A geographic lens for learning, prayer and informed action

The 10/40 Window

Explore a broad region where mission researchers have identified many communities with limited access to the gospel. The map is a starting point for learning, not a label for any person or a measure of spiritual need.

Notice the need. Learn the story. Listen first.

The 10/40 Window is commonly described as 10°–40° north latitude, stretching roughly from West Africa to East Asia. It includes many countries, religions, languages and local Christian communities. It is not uniformly unreached, and geography alone cannot tell us where a worker is needed.

Christian mission research uses people-group categories to identify gaps in access. Those labels describe a research framework, not a community’s worth, a person’s faith, or a call for outsiders to arrive. Start with local churches and partners; prayer, learning, giving, translation and practical support can all be meaningful responses.

Source review checklist

  1. Open the original. Confirm the publisher and that the link is the underlying source, not a summary.
  2. Check dates. Separate publication date, event date, reference year and retrieval date.
  3. Read definitions. Confirm what the indicator or category actually measures.
  4. Compare carefully. Look for corroboration and note differences in methods or coverage.
  5. Record uncertainty. Label direct observation, estimate, reported allegation, analysis or unresolved claim.
  6. Recheck before presenting. News and safety information can change; research snapshots may not.

Before you say yes

  1. Start with your church. Invite trusted leaders into discernment and ask how the organization works with local churches.
  2. Listen to local partners. Ask what they request, how they make decisions and whether the role serves an existing priority.
  3. Understand the commitment. Confirm length, training, costs and fundraising, support, visa and safety guidance with the organization.
  4. Ask about care and accountability. Review safeguarding, supervision, insurance, grievance processes and follow-up after service.
  5. Consider serving from home. Translation support, prayer, giving, professional help and learning can be valuable ways to participate.

How to use these profiles

The biographies summarize each organization’s own public description. Links lead to official organization pages. They do not confirm current vacancies, destination access, safety, local partner requests or fit for a particular person. Compare theology, practices and accountability; ask local believers what support is welcome.
